Kay Christofferson has served House District 53 since 2013 and chairs the House Transportation Committee. Three things stand out in his record: his campaign takes credit for a 2026 tax cut, but the bill he actually sponsored for it never passed, killed by the same midnight procedural mechanism that ended seven other bills that session; a wide-ranging transit bill he carried passed over organized public opposition; and a campaign narrative about his engineering career that his own signed disclosure forms don't fully support.

What this timeline actually supports

He genuinely worked at Horrocks in 2015–2016. He was self-employed by 2018. Horrocks' own company blog confirms it held the "I-15 Tech Corridor" contract (Triumph Boulevard, a frontage road system, interchange redesigns) — a project outlined in 2018 and largely completed by 2020. Christofferson's campaign site separately claims credit for accelerating funding on a project described in nearly identical terms. The plain reading: by the time that project was actually being built, he was no longer a Horrocks employee. That rules out the strongest version of this finding — "he personally profited as a Horrocks officer on the exact project he championed." It does not rule out a narrower one: his own firm, Christofferson Engineering, could have been hired as an outside consultant or subcontractor on that same project. Leaving a company as an employee and later working for it as a paid consultant are two different things, and nothing in his disclosure forms distinguishes between them.

Questions worth asking him directly

His tax returns aren't public record, and his COI disclosures don't itemize client work, so these can't be answered from documents alone:

  • Has Christofferson Engineering, PLLC ever done consulting or subcontract work connected to Horrocks or to the I-15 Tech Corridor project specifically?
  • Does his firm have any current relationship to Horrocks' ongoing Mountain View Corridor contract — the other project he's personally claimed credit for?

"Self-employed" doesn't rule either out, and nothing in the public record answers them either way.

The Unresolved Amendment

S.B. 197, "Transportation Funding and Governance Amendments," rewrites the core statute (59-12-103) governing sales-tax increment distribution for every major tax-increment tool in this project's coverage — HTRZ, RDA, UFAIR, and the Point of the Mountain Authority. Christofferson was the House floor sponsor shepherding it through the House.

What HTRZ, RDA, and UFAIR mean — and why they're here
HTRZ — Housing and Transit Reinvestment Zone
A Utah program that lets a designated zone near a transit station capture a share of the growth in sales tax revenue generated there, using that money to fund infrastructure and housing rather than sending it to the state's general fund.
RDA — (Community) Redevelopment Agency
A local tax-increment financing tool that captures the growth in property or sales tax revenue within a designated redevelopment area to pay for improvements inside that same area.
UFAIR — Utah Fairpark Area Investment and Restoration District
Created by H.B. 562 in the 2024 legislative session, this district captures tax increment to fund restoration, preservation, and economic development around the Utah Fairpark area in Salt Lake City.
Why it matters here
All three tools work the same basic way: instead of new tax revenue in a growing area going to the state or county's general fund, it gets redirected to pay for projects inside that same zone. S.B. 197 amends the one statute that governs how that redirected money gets divided up statewide — so a change to it doesn't just affect one zone or one project, it changes the math for every HTRZ, RDA, and UFAIR district in Utah at once. That's why the unrecorded base-year amendment below is a bigger deal than a routine transportation bill tweak.
Does this square with his own campaign promises?

Two separate campaign pages are worth holding up against this bill:

"Keeping Utah Affordable" — cutting taxes, putting money back in your pocket
What the record shows HTRZ, RDA, and UFAIR all work by redirecting the growth in sales tax revenue away from general government funds — the same pool that pays for schools, counties, and public services, or that could otherwise fund broader tax relief — into specific development zones instead. That's not a tax increase, but it is a different use of new tax dollars than his tax-cut messaging emphasizes, and his campaign materials don't mention this side of his tax-related work.
"Limited & Accountable Government" — transparent, understandable, accountable
What the record shows S.B. 197's base-year amendment — a change to how that redirected revenue gets divided up across every HTRZ, RDA, and UFAIR district in Utah — happened via an anonymous voice vote with no named mover, on a bill Christofferson personally floor-sponsored.

Whether either of these squares with the standard his own platform sets is a fair question to ask him directly.

H.B. 57 (2026) — Three Confirmed Financial Connections

Christofferson chief-sponsored H.B. 57, "Motor Vehicle Division Amendments" (floor sponsor Wayne A. Harper), which was signed into law. This bill drew a parallel to H.B. 22's pattern — three named entities: Maverik, Clyde Companies, and Lincoln Hill Partners.

What did the $16,000 to Gathering Inc. actually buy?

Full 2018–2026 campaign finance data confirms all three entities named in the original flag: Maverik is a recurring donor ($1,250 combined across multiple years, not a single instance); Clyde Companies is both a donor and, per his 2016 disclosure, a personal stock-redemption income source. The strongest piece is new: Christofferson's campaign paid $16,000 to "Gathering Inc." — $7,500 in 2020 and $8,500 in 2022, both listed only as "campaign expense," with no further detail. Gathering Inc. is an entity already tied to Casey Hill in this project's Election Hive/Lincoln Hill research — a registered lobbyist and Lincoln Hill PAC co-founder. This is money flowing out of the campaign to a network-connected vendor, not a donation coming in. What that $16,000 actually purchased isn't disclosed anywhere in the public record.

Christofferson carried this in the House; Wayne Harper chief-sponsored it in the Senate. The Utah House's own 2026 recap article calls it the session's most wide-ranging transit bill. It went through at least four substitutes and was signed into law.

Why did this bill pass despite organized opposition?

At the Senate committee hearing, a coalition testified against this bill: Dave Iltis (President, Cycling Utah), Julia Lyon (President, Wasatch Elementary PTA), Julian Jurkoic (Treasurer, Sweet Streets Salt Lake City), and at least six named residents. Only one witness — a Salt Lake City Council Member — spoke in favor. The bill passed anyway. On the floor, opposition wasn't purely partisan: the House vote (55–14–6) saw four Republicans cross over (Abbott, Gwynn, Ward, Welton), and the Senate's final concurrence vote (18–9–2) mixed Democratic and Republican Nays.

Graded Against the Rubric

Every finding on this page is graded the same five ways used across all Weber County Hive coverage: Power, Transparency, Financial accountability, Environmental impact, and Community impact.

S.B. 197's unrecorded base-year amendment D
Power — A two-year delay to a fund's base and trigger year is a substantive change to how and when tax-increment revenue gets captured — decided by voice vote with no name attached, while the bill's own House floor sponsor was Christofferson.
Transparency — Bad. The status-table record of this bill does not show who moved the amendment. A change this specific to a statewide tax mechanism deserves a named mover on the record.
Financial accountability — Bad. This statute underlies every major TIF-style tool in this project's coverage; a two-year delay to its base year affects when public entities start seeing, or not seeing, revenue.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Bad. Residents and taxing entities affected by HTRZ, RDA, UFAIR, and Point of the Mountain financing have no way to know from the public record who decided to push this timeline back.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterAs the bill's floor sponsor, Christofferson was in the room. A voice vote doesn't preclude naming who proposed an amendment in the sponsor's own summary of the bill's changes when presenting it — that's a choice available regardless of how the vote itself was conducted.
S.B. 242 — passed over real, organized public opposition C
Power — Mixed. Some provisions genuinely streamline transit funding and infrastructure delivery; the mobile-business and deed-conveyance riders added late in the process don't obviously belong in a transportation bill and got far less scrutiny as a result.
Transparency — Good, on balance. Unlike the S.B. 197 amendment, this bill's committee process included real, named, public testimony on both sides — the record of who supported and opposed it, and why, is actually there to check.
Financial accountability — Mixed. Corridor preservation funding now flows more directly to local governments, which the bill's authors frame as removing delay — but the local-option sales tax changes and the late-added riders haven't been independently assessed for who bears the cost.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Bad. A coalition of bike/pedestrian-safety and neighborhood advocates — Cycling Utah, a PTA, a complete-streets group — testified against this bill and lost. Their specific objections weren't addressed in the final text as far as the record shows.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterSeparating the late-added, unrelated riders (mobile business rules, the deed provision) into their own bills would have let each get proper public scrutiny instead of riding through on a "wide-ranging" transit bill's momentum.
H.B. 235 — his own bill, killed the same way as seven others D
Power — Bad. A bill with real, recorded bipartisan-adjacent support at every stage — House floor, Senate committee — was killed by a procedural mechanism at the last possible moment, with no debate or explanation on the record.
Transparency — Bad. The kill mechanism itself (enacting-clause strike) requires no floor debate and no stated reason — voters have no way to know why a bill with this much support didn't become law.
Financial accountability — Mixed. The tax cut itself happened anyway through an unnamed alternate vehicle — but Christofferson's own campaign materials never disclose that his personal bill for it failed, crediting "the Legislature" generally instead.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Mixed. Utah taxpayers got the rate cut regardless of this bill's fate, so the practical outcome for most residents is the same — the concern here is about accurate credit-taking, not a lost benefit.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterA campaign page taking credit for a policy outcome could simply say which specific bill delivered it — if that wasn't his own H.B. 235, saying so plainly would cost nothing and would be more accurate than crediting "the Legislature" in the abstract.
H.B. 57 and the $16,000 to Gathering Inc. C
Power — Mixed. A chief sponsor whose campaign paid $16,000 to a vendor connected to the Election Hive/Lincoln Hill network is worth scrutiny, but bill sponsorship and vendor payment are two separate acts — this shows a financial relationship, not proof the bill itself was shaped by it.
Transparency — Bad. Both $16,000 payments are logged only as "campaign expense" — no description of what was purchased. That's within disclosure rules but tells the public nothing.
Financial accountability — Bad. All three entities named in the original Election Hive flag for this bill — Maverik, Clyde Companies, and Gathering Inc. — are confirmed financial relationships, not a coincidental pattern match to a different bill.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Mixed. Without knowing what the $16,000 purchased, it's not possible to say whether the public interest was affected — only that the financial relationship exists and isn't explained.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterA specific expenditure description — even a general one like "consulting services" or "polling" — would let the public understand what a $16,000 payment was actually for, rather than a generic label that discloses nothing.
The Horrocks/I-15 overlap TOO WEAK TO GRADE
This one doesn't get a full rubric grade. His own disclosure forms show he was no longer a Horrocks employee when the I-15 Tech Corridor project was built, which undercuts the strongest version of this finding. But leaving as an employee doesn't rule out working the same project as a paid outside consultant through his own firm — that possibility is untested, not closed. What's confirmed either way: his trade association funds his campaign, and he's claimed personal credit for two separate Horrocks-built projects in his own campaign materials. That's a pattern worth noting, not a documented conflict.

Overall grade, as a candidate

C-

This reflects two real, concrete accountability problems (the anonymous S.B. 197 amendment and H.B. 235's no-debate kill) and a recurring pattern of campaign messaging that doesn't name the specific bill or relationship behind a claim — balanced against genuinely thorough COI disclosure and real public hearings on S.B. 242 that most of this project's other subjects haven't gotten. This grade can change as more facts come in. Several threads on this page are still open — what the $16,000 to Gathering Inc. actually purchased, whether Christofferson Engineering has done any work connected to Horrocks, who moved the S.B. 197 amendment — and any of them could move this grade in either direction once resolved.

Where This Stands

H.B. 235's confirmed midnight kill and S.B. 242's genuine public opposition are the two strongest, most concrete findings on this page — both grounded in primary vote records and committee testimony. The $16,000 to Gathering Inc. is the strongest lead in the H.B. 57 thread, though what that money purchased remains unknown. The S.B. 197 amendment mystery is substantive but likely unresolvable without direct access to the written House Journal. The Horrocks/I-15 overlap is weak and is kept here as background, not a leading finding.
