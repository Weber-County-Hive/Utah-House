Kay Christofferson has served House District 53 since 2013. He chairs the House Transportation Committee. Three things stand out in his record. First, his campaign takes credit for a 2026 tax cut, but the bill he sponsored for it never passed. It was killed by the same midnight move that ended seven other bills that session. Second, a broad transit bill he carried passed even though organized groups opposed it. Third, his campaign tells a story about his engineering career that his own signed disclosure forms do not fully back up.

What this timeline actually supports

He did work at Horrocks in 2015–2016. He was self-employed by 2018. Horrocks' own company blog says it held the "I-15 Tech Corridor" contract (Triumph Boulevard, a frontage road system and new interchange designs). That project was planned in 2018 and mostly finished by 2020. Christofferson's campaign site takes credit for speeding up funding for a project described in almost the same words. Put simply: by the time that project was being built, he no longer worked for Horrocks. That rules out the strongest version of this finding, that he made money as a Horrocks officer on the same project he pushed. It does not rule out a smaller version. His own firm, Christofferson Engineering, could have been hired as an outside consultant or subcontractor on that project. Leaving a company as an employee and later working for it as a paid consultant are two different things. Nothing on his disclosure forms tells them apart.

Questions worth asking him directly

His tax returns are not public, and his disclosure forms do not list client work. So these questions cannot be answered from documents alone:

  • Has Christofferson Engineering, PLLC ever done consulting or subcontract work tied to Horrocks, or to the I-15 Tech Corridor project in particular?
  • Does his firm have any current tie to Horrocks' Mountain View Corridor contract? That is the other project he has taken credit for.

"Self-employed" doesn't rule either out, and nothing in the public record answers them either way.

The Unresolved Amendment

S.B. 197, "Transportation Funding and Governance Amendments," rewrites the main law (59-12-103) on how sales-tax increment is split up. That law covers every big tax-increment tool this project tracks: HTRZ, RDA, UFAIR and the Point of the Mountain Authority. Christofferson carried it through the House as its floor sponsor.

What HTRZ, RDA, and UFAIR mean — and why they're here
HTRZ — Housing and Transit Reinvestment Zone
A Utah program that lets a designated zone near a transit station capture a share of the growth in sales tax revenue generated there, using that money to fund infrastructure and housing rather than sending it to the state's general fund.
RDA — (Community) Redevelopment Agency
A local tax-increment financing tool that captures the growth in property or sales tax revenue within a designated redevelopment area to pay for improvements inside that same area.
UFAIR — Utah Fairpark Area Investment and Restoration District
Created by H.B. 562 in the 2024 legislative session, this district captures tax increment to fund restoration, preservation, and economic development around the Utah Fairpark area in Salt Lake City.
Why it matters here
All three tools work the same basic way: instead of new tax revenue in a growing area going to the state or county's general fund, it gets redirected to pay for projects inside that same zone. S.B. 197 amends the one statute that governs how that redirected money gets divided up statewide — so a change to it doesn't just affect one zone or one project, it changes the math for every HTRZ, RDA, and UFAIR district in Utah at once. That's why the unrecorded base-year amendment below is a bigger deal than a routine transportation bill tweak.
Does this square with his own campaign promises?

Two separate campaign pages are worth holding up against this bill:

"Keeping Utah Affordable" — cutting taxes, putting money back in your pocket
What the record shows HTRZ, RDA and UFAIR all work by sending the growth in sales tax money to certain development zones. Without them, that money would go to the general pot that pays for schools, counties and public services, or could pay for wider tax relief. That is not a tax increase. But it is a different use of new tax dollars than his tax-cut message stresses. His campaign materials do not mention this side of his tax work.
"Limited & Accountable Government" — transparent, understandable, accountable
What the record shows S.B. 197's base-year amendment changed how that money is split across every HTRZ, RDA and UFAIR district in Utah. It passed by a voice vote, with no name listed for who moved it. Christofferson was the bill's floor sponsor.

Whether either of these fits the standard his own platform sets is a fair question to ask.

What "Wayne Harper Network" means

H.B. 57 (2026) — Three Confirmed Financial Connections

Christofferson was the primary sponsor of H.B. 57, "Motor Vehicle Division Amendments" (floor sponsor Wayne A. Harper), which was signed into law. This bill drew a parallel to H.B. 22's pattern — three named entities: Maverik, Clyde Companies, and Lincoln Hill Partners.

What did the $16,000 to Gathering Inc. actually buy?

His full 2018–2026 campaign finance records confirm all three businesses named in the first flag. Maverik gave more than once ($1,250 in all over several years). Clyde Companies is both a donor and, per his 2016 disclosure, a source of personal income from a stock redemption. The strongest new piece: his campaign paid $16,000 to "Gathering Inc." That was $7,500 in 2020 and $8,500 in 2022. Both are listed only as "campaign expense," with no other detail. This project's Election Hive and Lincoln Hill research ties Gathering Inc. to Casey Hill, a registered lobbyist and a co-founder of Lincoln Hill PAC. This is money going out of the campaign to a vendor in that network, not a donation coming in. What the $16,000 bought is not shown anywhere in the public record.

Why did this bill pass despite organized opposition?

At the Senate committee hearing, a group spoke against this bill. It included Dave Iltis (President, Cycling Utah), Julia Lyon (President, Wasatch Elementary PTA), Julian Jurkoic (Treasurer, Sweet Streets Salt Lake City) and at least six named residents. Only one person, a Salt Lake City Council member, spoke for it. The bill passed anyway. On the floor, the no votes did not all come from one party. In the House vote (55–14–6), four Republicans voted no (Abbott, Gwynn, Ward, Welton). The Senate's final vote (18–9–2) had no votes from both parties.

Graded Against the Rubric

Every finding on this page is graded the same five ways we use on all Hive pages: Power, Transparency, Financial accountability, Environmental impact and Community impact.

S.B. 197's unrecorded base-year amendment D
Power — Pushing a fund's base year and trigger year back two years is a real change. It changes how and when tax-increment money is captured. It was decided by voice vote with no name attached. Christofferson was the bill's House floor sponsor.
Transparency — The bill's status record does not show who moved the amendment. A change this specific to a statewide tax tool should have a named mover on the record.
Financial accountability — This law is behind every major tax-increment tool this project tracks. Pushing the base year back two years changes when public bodies start getting, or not getting, that money.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — People and taxing bodies affected by HTRZ, RDA, UFAIR and Point of the Mountain money cannot tell from the public record who decided to push this date back.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterAs the floor sponsor, Christofferson was in the room. Even with a voice vote, he could have said who proposed the amendment when he explained the bill's changes. That was his choice to make, no matter how the vote was taken.
S.B. 242 — passed over real, organized public opposition C
Power — Some parts really do speed up transit funding and building. But the mobile-business and property-deed add-ons came late. They do not clearly belong in a transportation bill, and they got much less review because of that.
Transparency — Good, overall. Unlike the S.B. 197 amendment, this bill's committee hearing had real, named, public testimony on both sides. The record of who backed it, who opposed it and why is there to check.
Financial accountability — Money to save land for future roads now goes more directly to local governments. The bill's authors say this removes delay. But no one outside has checked who pays for the local sales tax changes and the late add-ons.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Bike and pedestrian-safety groups and neighborhood groups spoke against this bill. They included Cycling Utah, a PTA and a "complete streets" group. They lost. As far as the record shows, the final bill did not answer their specific concerns.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterThe late, unrelated add-ons (mobile-business rules and the deed clause) could have been their own bills. Then each would have gotten proper public review instead of riding along on a broad transit bill.
H.B. 235 — his own bill, killed the same way as seven others D
Power — The bill had real recorded support at every step, from both parties or close to it, on the House floor and in Senate committee. It was killed by a procedural move at the last moment, with no debate and no reason on the record.
Transparency — The way it was killed (striking the enacting clause) needs no floor debate and no stated reason. Voters cannot know why a bill with this much support did not become law.
Financial accountability — The tax cut still happened, through another bill that his page does not name. His campaign materials never say his own bill for it failed. They credit "the Legislature" instead.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Utah taxpayers got the rate cut either way, so the result for most people is the same. The concern here is about honest credit, not a lost benefit.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterA campaign page taking credit for a result could name the bill that delivered it. If it was not his own H.B. 235, saying so would cost nothing. It would be more accurate than crediting "the Legislature" in general.
H.B. 57 and the $16,000 to Gathering Inc. C
Power — A primary sponsor whose campaign paid $16,000 to a vendor tied to the Election Hive and Lincoln Hill network deserves a closer look. But sponsoring a bill and paying a vendor are two separate acts. This shows a money tie. It does not prove the tie shaped the bill.
Transparency — Both payments are listed only as "campaign expense," with no word on what was bought. That meets the disclosure rules but tells the public nothing.
Financial accountability — All three businesses named in the first Election Hive flag for this bill (Maverik, Clyde Companies and Gathering Inc.) have confirmed money ties to him. It is not a chance match to some other bill.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Without knowing what the $16,000 bought, we cannot say whether the public was affected. We can only say the money tie exists and is not explained.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterA short description of the spending, even a general one like "consulting services" or "polling," would tell the public what a $16,000 payment was for. A generic label tells them nothing.
The Horrocks/I-15 overlap TOO WEAK TO GRADE
This one does not get a full rubric grade. His own disclosure forms show he no longer worked for Horrocks when the I-15 Tech Corridor project was built. That weakens the strongest version of this finding. But leaving as an employee does not rule out working on the same project as a paid outside consultant through his own firm. That has not been checked; it is not ruled out. What is confirmed either way: his trade group funds his campaign, and he has taken personal credit for two different Horrocks-built projects in his campaign materials. That is a pattern worth noting, not a proven conflict.

As a Candidate: What This Means for the Collective

Overall grade, as a candidate

C-

This grade reflects two real accountability problems: the unnamed S.B. 197 amendment and H.B. 235's no-debate death. It also reflects a repeated pattern of campaign messages that do not name the bill or tie behind a claim. On the other side, his disclosure forms are more complete than most, and S.B. 242 got real public hearings that most other subjects of this project have not. This grade can change as more facts come in. Several questions are still open: what the $16,000 to Gathering Inc. bought, whether Christofferson Engineering has done work tied to Horrocks, and who moved the S.B. 197 amendment. Any answer could move this grade either way.

Where This Stands

H.B. 235's confirmed midnight kill and S.B. 242's genuine public opposition are the two strongest, most concrete findings on this page — both grounded in primary vote records and committee testimony. The $16,000 to Gathering Inc. is the strongest lead in the H.B. 57 thread, though what that money purchased remains unknown. The S.B. 197 amendment mystery is substantive but likely unresolvable without direct access to the written House Journal. The Horrocks/I-15 overlap is weak and is kept here as background, not a leading finding.
