In plain words

Thurston represented Utah House District 62 (Provo / Springville, Utah County). He began serving January 1, 2015. This page shows what his own forms, bills and campaign reports say. He lost his seat on Nov. 3, 2026; the page stays up so readers can follow his record going forward.

Graded Against the Rubric

Every finding is graded the same five ways used across Hive coverage: Power, Transparency, Financial accountability, Environmental impact and Community impact.

The conflict of interest form
Power — His form lists two family businesses and one national board seat.
Transparency — His 2025 and 2026 forms list the same employer, businesses and board seat. The only change is his spouse's employer.
Financial accountability — The form lists sources, not amounts, so the size of each interest is not shown.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — His employer, the National Association of Health Data Organizations, works on health data. He was the primary sponsor of H.B. 199, Health Data Amendments, which passed.
What could have been done differentlyItem 8A lets an officeholder describe any other possible conflict. He wrote "None." A line there about his health-data work and H.B. 199 would have put the two side by side for readers.
Campaign money
Power — His largest source was his own deposits ($24,755.46), most labeled as reimbursements. The largest outside donor was the Utah House Republican Election Committee PAC ($5,500).
Transparency — Richard Jaussi is listed as both a donor ($5,496.11) and a payee ($4,826.58). $4,209.80 of payments list no payee name.
Financial accountability — Travel and lodging are his largest spending category ($24,665.93), including $19,821.77 at The Kimball in Salt Lake City.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Voters can see who gave and who was paid.
What could have been done differentlyPayments with a blank payee name could list who was paid. The state report has a purpose field for each payment. Filling it with what was bought (printing, texts, consulting) would answer what "Campaign Expense" paid for.
2026 bills
Power — H.B. 74 (signature gathering) and H.B. 231 (repeal of the county restaurant tax) did not pass. Six of his nine passed.
Transparency — H.B. 199 changes the state health department's health data authority.
Financial accountability — H.B. 22 (classic vehicle designation) is the only one of his bills that moves money: $36,400 in transfers.
Environmental impact — None of his bills changes land, water or air rules.
Community impact — H.B. 74 would have changed rules for initiative and referendum petitions; it did not pass. The Hive's petition-laws page tracks it.
What could have been done differentlyNot applicable.

Three Ways to Look at It

1. The auditor's look: do the records line up?

  • Reports were filed for each year shown (2018–2026). The totals come from one pull on Nov. 26, 2026, with duplicate and deleted lines removed.
  • $4,209.80 in payments list no payee name.
  • Richard Jaussi appears on both sides of the ledger.
  • Transfers between his own accounts ($53,469.09 over 2018, 2020 and 2022) are counted once.

2. The collective rubric: is it good for everyone?

See the grade cards above. Each card ends with what could have been done differently, based only on what the record leaves open.

3. The Legislature's view: what the law asks, and how lawmakers voted

  • The form follows state law. Utah Code 20A-11-1604 sets what goes on the conflict of interest form. It asks for sources, not amounts, and Item 8A is optional.
  • Campaign reports follow state law. Each line shows a date, name, amount and a purpose label chosen by the campaign.
  • Six of his nine bills passed; H.B. 74, H.B. 231 and H.B. 505 did not.
  • He also carried five Senate bills in the House.
