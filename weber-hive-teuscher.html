House District 44 · Profile

Rep. Jordan D. Teuscher

House Rules Chair · Co-Chair, Point of the Mountain State Land Authority

A conflict-of-interest form that lists no board seats. A board he's personally legislated for three sessions running. A bill he held with no stated reason. A sign-disclosure law with a real money trail underneath it.

Jordan Teuscher chairs the most powerful procedural committee in the Utah House. He also co-chairs a state land authority worth hundreds of millions of dollars in development. His own signed disclosure form doesn't mention the second job at all — and that's just the starting point.

The Board He Legislates For

The Point of the Mountain State Land Authority oversees one of Utah's largest active development projects — the former state prison site in Draper, now being built out into housing, offices, and infrastructure worth hundreds of millions of dollars. Teuscher has sat on its board as Co-Chair (the House Speaker's appointee to the seat) since at least 2022.

The Bill He Held

In 2026, H.B. 332 would have required a school district to get voter approval before taking on a specific kind of construction debt — closing a gap that let one Utah district borrow $201 million without ever putting it to a vote. At its February 13, 2026 committee hearing, three school-district administrators testified against it. No one testified in favor.

Immediately after that testimony, Teuscher personally moved to substitute the bill, then moved to hold it. Both motions passed 12–0. No reason was given for either, on the record. The bill sat frozen for three weeks, then was struck from the books at 11:59 p.m. on the last night of the session — without ever getting a floor vote.

What the record does and doesn't show

Public records show the testimony and the motions, in that order, from the same meeting. They don't show why. State the sequence, not a motive.

The Bill He Weakened, Twice

H.B. 197 (2021), Teuscher's first-session bill, was billed as "improving the integrity of Utah's primary election system by preventing party raiding." What the bill comparison documents show is different: Teuscher personally proposed a later substitute that stripped out two anti-raiding provisions present in his own earlier draft — including one closing a loophole where a voter could deregister and re-register specifically to dodge the bill's own deadline.

The substitution that removed those provisions happened by voice vote. No names, no recorded count. The bill itself wasn't a rubber stamp — it passed the House 41–30, with real opposition — which makes the unrecorded content change more notable, not less.

The Sign Bill and the Money Behind It

H.B. 33 (2026), "Political Signs Amendments," exempts small campaign signs from disclosure requirements and — in its enacted form — dropped a requirement that disclosure text actually be legible up close. Teuscher chief-sponsored it while receiving money from his own PAC. The bill's Senate sponsor paid over $4,500 to a firm tied to state political infrastructure the same day a Delaware shell entity with a UPS Store mailbox for an address made same-day donations to two different candidates, including Teuscher.

Graded Against the Rubric

Every finding on this page is graded the same five ways, applied consistently across all Weber County Hive coverage: Power (does it add or remove a check the public would otherwise have no say over?), Transparency (can the public actually see the process?), Financial accountability (who pays, and is that disclosed plainly?), Environmental impact, and Community impact (who bears the cost, and did they get real standing to be heard?).

The undisclosed POMSLA board seat
Power — He personally reshapes the structure of a board he sits on, three sessions running, without the public ever being told the two roles are held by the same person.
Transparency — The one document specifically designed to surface this — his own signed COI form — lists the seat as N/A. The public has no official record of the connection at all.
Financial accountability — The Authority moves hundreds of millions of dollars in development financing; who sits on its board and who benefits isn't something a resident can check against his own disclosures.
Environmental impact — Not directly applicable to the disclosure gap itself; the Authority's own land-use decisions are a separate, unassessed question.
Community impact — Residents near the development have no way of knowing, from his official record, that their representative also governs the project directly.
How This Could Have Served the Public BetterFiling a truthful board-position disclosure costs nothing and takes five minutes. Utah's COI form already has the exact field for this. The fix isn't a new law — it's using the one that already exists as written.
H.B. 332 — held with no stated reason
Power — The bill would have shifted power toward voters on school district debt. Holding it preserved the status quo without anyone having to defend that choice.
Transparency — The hearing itself was public and on the record. The decision to freeze the bill, made in the same breath, came with no stated reasoning at all.
Financial accountability — A bill aimed at making large district debt harder to take on without a vote died without a floor vote of its own.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — The residents who'd bear the cost of future no-vote district borrowing had no voice in the moment the protection was shelved.
How This Could Have Served the Public BetterA bill that clears committee with no recorded opposition deserves an up-or-down floor vote. If there was a real objection, stating it on the record costs nothing and gives the public an actual answer.
H.B. 33 — the sign-disclosure carve-outs and the money behind them
Power — No direct power shift, but the bill's exemptions make it harder for the public to know who is speaking to them in an election.
Transparency — The enacted version dropped a legibility requirement for disclosure text and exempts small signs from disclosure entirely — both reduce, not increase, what a voter can actually see.
Financial accountability — Real money moved through a Delaware shell entity with a UPS Store address and an advertising vendor with no findable business registration, timed around the bill's own legislative window.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Voters bear the cost of not knowing who funded the political speech in front of them; well-funded campaigns bear none of the disclosure burden the exemptions remove.
How This Could Have Served the Public BetterKeep the legibility requirement for every sign, not just billboards, and set the small-sign disclosure exemption low enough that it can't be used to route PAC money through a technically-compliant but effectively invisible disclosure.

By the Numbers

Across six sessions (2021–2026), Teuscher has sponsored or floor-sponsored roughly 111 bills. Sorting them by which of his own campaign platform's twelve stated priorities they actually serve shows a real gap between what he campaigns on and what he legislates.

What He Campaigns On vs. What He Legislates

Categorization is our own grouping of his bills by subject, not an official taxonomy — see the sources list for the full bill-by-bill breakdown.

The gap

His platform names "Protecting Utah's Resources" and "Improving Air Quality" as standing priorities. Across six sessions, exactly one bill touches air quality — a vehicle emissions inspection enforcement bill, not a clean-air measure — and none touch natural resources specifically. "Supporting Legal Immigration & Rule of Law" is a named platform section with zero bills behind it. Meanwhile "Government Accountability," his most-cited campaign theme, is also his single largest legislative category — and includes the three Point of the Mountain bills already documented above as the undisclosed board conflict.

Bills Brought Forward or Carried, by Year

A steady, rising workload — floor-sponsorships (Senate bills he personally carries in the House) have grown to roughly match his own bills. 2025 was his heaviest year yet, at 29 bills total.

Campaign priorities with little or no legislative record behind them

Transparency — A campaign platform is a public promise. When a stated priority produces zero or one bill across six years, voters have no easy way to see that gap unless someone counts it for them — it isn't disclosed or flagged anywhere in his own materials.
Financial accountability — Not directly applicable to this specific finding.
Environmental impact — Directly relevant here: "Protecting Utah's Resources" and "Improving Air Quality" are named platform priorities with essentially no corresponding legislative action across six sessions.
Community impact — Constituents who voted based on stated environmental or immigration priorities have six years of record to check those promises against — and the record shows little activity in those specific areas.
How This Could Have Served the Public BetterA campaign platform doesn't obligate a specific bill count in every category — priorities can be pursued through committee work, budget votes, or supporting someone else's bill, not just personal sponsorship. But when a section of a platform produces essentially nothing sponsorship-wise for six straight years, saying so plainly — "this is a value I hold, not a legislative focus area" — would be more honest than leaving the platform page unchanged year after year.

His Vote on Other People's Bills

His own sponsored bills show what he brings forward. These four votes show what he does when someone else writes the bill and he just has to decide yes, no, or absent.

H.B. 249 (2025) — voted Yea, with the entire Republican caucus D
Power — Enables a private developer's nuclear-adjacent infrastructure financing through a tax-formula change most voters never see debated in those terms.
Transparency — The vote itself was recorded and public; what the bill actually does to the tax rate formula is technical enough that most voters couldn't follow it from the vote alone.
Financial accountability — Creates "phantom increment" conditions that shift real cost onto school districts, without a plain statement of who pays.
Environmental impact — Not fully assessed here; nuclear-adjacent siting deserves its own separate review.
Community impact — School districts and the communities they serve bear a cost they didn't get a clear, upfront accounting of.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterA vote isn't all-or-nothing. He could have voted yes on the infrastructure piece while pushing, on the record, for the tax-formula change to be split out and studied separately — the same objection some Democratic colleagues raised in floor debate. He didn't have to cast the deciding vote to ask a hard question out loud.
H.B. 85 (2026) — voted Yea in committee and on the House floor N/A
What it actually does — Bars the governor or a local chief executive from declaring a state of emergency exclusively because the UN, World Economic Forum, or WHO proclaimed one, and declares those bodies have no legal authority in Utah. No appropriation, no enforcement mechanism beyond that declaration.
Power — Narrow and largely symbolic — international bodies have no binding domestic authority under U.S. law regardless, so this mostly restates existing reality rather than shifting real power.
Transparency — Neutral; doesn't change what the public can see about any process.
Financial accountability — Not applicable — no money involved.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Minimal, given the narrow and largely symbolic scope.
What's still worth notingThe committee vote (6-4-2) looked like real cross-party disagreement, but it didn't hold: on the House floor, final passage was 55-11-9, with every single Nay coming from a Democrat. Two of the four committee dissenters, Walter and Ward, flipped to Yea on the floor; only Arthur and Fitisemanu stayed consistent. The bill cleared the House but ultimately died anyway ("House file for bills not passed," last action 3/6/2026) — since it passed the House, it must have stalled in the Senate, Sen. Winterton's own chamber as the bill's floor sponsor. Worth a follow-up: why would a bill's own floor sponsor's chamber let it die after the House had already passed it 55-11?
H.B. 37 (2025) — absent on the deciding vote D
Power — Bad by omission. The bill quietly lowered the incorporation threshold feeding the preliminary-municipality fast-track already documented elsewhere in this project. Not voting isn't neutral when the final margin (39-30) was close enough that his vote could have mattered.
Transparency — Bad. An absence on a contested, narrow-margin vote gives the public nothing to check him against — no yes to defend, no no to explain.
Financial accountability — Not directly applicable.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Bad. Residents of unincorporated areas affected by the lowered threshold had no way to know where their own representative stood.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterBeing present and voting no would have cost him nothing politically that an absence didn't also cost him — and it would have given constituents an actual position to hold him to.
H.B. 17 (2026) — a narrow exception swapped for a broad one D+
RESOLVED, from the bill's own floor-amendment markup — The original introduced bill let a PID board meet outside its own boundaries only for an annual "retreat": once per calendar year, for planning/education purposes, held as a public meeting, with the board barred from taking final action or voting on anything but adjournment. A floor amendment struck that entire narrow exception and replaced it with one line: a board may meet anywhere "within the boundaries of the creating entity" — no annual limit, no purpose restriction, no bar on taking real votes. Every recorded vote on this bill, at every stage, was effectively unanimous (committee 11-0-1, House floor 70-0-5, Senate 26-0-3), so Teuscher's vote was "yes" on the swap.
Power — Bad. A PID board can now meet, and take binding votes, anywhere within an entire city or county — not just a once-a-year retreat with voting barred.
Transparency — Bad. What looks on its face like "removing a loophole" (striking the retreat provision) actually removed the SAFEGUARDS around meeting outside district boundaries, not the ability to do so.
Financial accountability — Not directly applicable to this specific provision.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Bad. Residents of a PID with no residents of their own (common for PIDs on undeveloped land) already have limited standing; this makes it easier for board meetings to happen even further from anyone actually affected.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterKeep the original retreat exception's actual safeguards (annual limit, no final votes) and simply widen where it can be held, rather than trading a narrow-but-safe exception for a broad one with no comparable limits at all.
