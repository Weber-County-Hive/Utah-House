House District 44 · Profile

Rep. Jordan D. Teuscher

House Rules Chair · Co-Chair, Point of the Mountain State Land Authority

A conflict-of-interest form that lists no board seats. A board he's personally legislated for three sessions running. A bill he held with no stated reason. A sign-disclosure law that changed which signs must say who paid.

The Weber County Hive South Jordan / Riverton, Salt Lake County In office since January 2021

Posted Sept. 8, 2026 · Updated Nov. 26, 2026 · Page look updated Nov. 2, 2026

Jordan Teuscher leads the House Rules Committee, one of the most powerful committees in the Utah House. He also co-chairs the board of a state land authority that runs a large building project. His own signed disclosure form does not mention the board job at all. That is just the start.

The Board He Legislates For

The Point of the Mountain State Land Authority runs one of Utah's biggest building projects. It is on the old state prison land in Draper. Housing, offices, roads and pipes are being built there. A $165 million state loan fund pays for the roads and pipes (board minutes, 2023). Teuscher has been a co-chair of its board since January 2023. The House Speaker named him to the seat.

The Bill He Held

In 2026, H.B. 332 would have made school districts get voter approval before taking on one kind of building debt. That would have closed a gap that let one Utah district borrow $201 million without a vote. At the Feb. 13, 2026 committee hearing, three school-district leaders spoke against it. No one spoke for it.

Immediately after that testimony, Teuscher personally moved to substitute the bill, then moved to hold it. Both motions passed 12–0. No reason was given for either, on the record. The bill sat frozen for three weeks, then was struck from the books at 11:59 p.m. on the last night of the session — without ever getting a floor vote.

What the record does and doesn't show

Public records show the testimony and the motions, in that order, from the same meeting. They don't show why. State the sequence, not a motive.

The Bill He Weakened, Twice

H.B. 197 (2021) was Teuscher's first-session bill. His campaign site says it was about "improving the integrity of Utah's primary election system by preventing party raiding." The bill's own comparison papers show something more. Teuscher proposed a later version that removed two anti-raiding rules from his own earlier drafts. One of them closed a gap: a voter could drop off the voter list and sign up again to get around the bill's deadline. The same version added one smaller rule. It covers voters who left a party after April 1 and then tried to rejoin one at the polls.

The substitution that removed those provisions happened by voice vote. No names, no recorded count. The bill passed the House 41–30, with 30 members voting no.

The Sign Bill and the Money Behind It

H.B. 33 (2026), "Political Signs Amendments," made the size limit smaller for campaign signs that can skip a "paid for by" line. It went from 4 by 8 feet to under 24 by 18 inches. The Senate's version was adopted by voice vote. It added a new pass: a sign a candidate pays for that names the candidate can skip the line. It also dropped a rule that the line be readable up close on ordinary signs. Teuscher was the primary sponsor. His campaign reports show $35,682 paid to Invencio (mostly labeled "Advertising"). They show $7,200 from Conservative Millennials PAC (2022–2026) and $5,000 from the Doers Network (2026). The reports do not tie this money to the bill.

Graded Against the Rubric

Every finding on this page is graded the same five ways we use on all Hive pages. Power: does it add or take away a check the public would not otherwise have? Transparency: can the public see how it was done? Financial accountability: who pays, and is that said plainly? Environmental impact. Community impact: who pays the cost, and did they get a real chance to be heard?

The POMSLA board seat not listed on his form
Power — Three sessions in a row, he carried bills that changed a board he sits on. The public was never told the same person held both roles.
Transparency — His signed conflict-of-interest form is the one paper meant to show this. It lists board seats as "N/A." There is no official record of the link.
Financial accountability — The authority handles a $165 million state loan fund for roads and pipes. A resident cannot check who sits on its board, or who gains, by reading his disclosures.
Environmental impact — Not directly part of the disclosure gap. The authority's own land-use choices are a separate question we have not graded.
Community impact — People who live near the project cannot tell from his official record that their representative also helps run it.
How This Could Have Served the Public BetterFiling a truthful board-position disclosure costs nothing and takes five minutes. Utah's COI form already has the exact field for this. The fix isn't a new law — it's using the one that already exists as written.
H.B. 332 — held with no stated reason
Power — The bill would have shifted power toward voters on school district debt. Holding it preserved the status quo without anyone having to defend that choice.
Transparency — The hearing itself was public and on the record. The decision to freeze the bill, made in the same breath, came with no stated reasoning at all.
Financial accountability — The bill would have made big school-district debt harder to take on without a vote. It died without its own floor vote.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — The people who would pay for future district borrowing without a vote had no say when the protection was set aside.
How This Could Have Served the Public BetterA bill that clears committee with no one on record against it should get a yes-or-no floor vote. If someone had a real objection, saying so on the record costs nothing. It gives the public a real answer.
H.B. 33 — the sign-disclosure rules
Power — New crimes and an infraction for sign conduct, plus new duties for cities and counties. Every final vote was recorded.
Transparency — Far more yard signs now need a "paid for by" line. But the final version lets signs a candidate pays for, that name the candidate, skip it. It also dropped the up-close readability rule. Both changes came in a version adopted by voice vote.
Financial accountability — His campaign paid Invencio $35,682, labeled mostly "Advertising"; the reports don't say what it bought.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — One statewide rule for park-strip signs and clean-up after elections.
How This Could Have Served the Public BetterA roll-call vote on the Senate version. And a smallest-type-size rule for the "paid for by" line on ordinary signs.

That is better than leaving the platform page the same year after year.

His Vote on Other People's Bills

His own sponsored bills show what he brings forward. These four votes show what he does when someone else writes the bill and he just has to decide yes, no, or absent.

H.B. 249 (2025) — voted Yea, with the entire Republican caucus D
Power — It helps a private developer pay for infrastructure tied to a nuclear project. It does this through a change to a tax formula. Most voters never saw it debated in those terms.
Transparency — The vote was recorded and public. But what the bill does to the tax formula is so technical that most voters could not follow it from the vote alone.
Financial accountability — It creates "phantom increment" conditions. That shifts real costs onto school districts. The bill does not say plainly who pays.
Environmental impact — Not fully assessed here; nuclear-adjacent siting deserves its own separate review.
Community impact — School districts and the families they serve carry a cost. They did not get a clear, up-front accounting of it.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterA vote is not all-or-nothing. He could have voted yes on the infrastructure part. He could also have asked, on the record, for the tax-formula change to be split out and studied on its own. Some Democrats raised that same objection in floor debate. He did not have to cast the deciding vote to ask a hard question out loud.
H.B. 85 (2026) — voted Yea in committee and on the House floor N/A
What it actually does — It stops the governor or a local leader from declaring an emergency only because the UN, the World Economic Forum or the World Health Organization declared one. It says those groups have no legal power in Utah. It spends no money. It has no way to enforce it beyond that statement.
Power — Narrow and mostly symbolic. Under U.S. law, international groups already have no binding power here. So it mostly restates what is already true.
Transparency — Neutral; doesn't change what the public can see about any process.
Financial accountability — Not applicable — no money involved.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Minimal, given the narrow and largely symbolic scope.
What's still worth notingThe committee vote (6-4-2) looked like real disagreement across parties. It did not last. On the House floor the bill passed 55-11-9, and every no vote came from a Democrat. Two of the four committee no votes, Walter and Ward, voted yes on the floor. Only Arthur and Fitisemanu voted no both times. The bill passed the House but died anyway ("House file for bills not passed," last action 3/6/2026). Since it passed the House, it must have stalled in the Senate. That is the chamber of Sen. Winterton, the bill's floor sponsor. A question to follow up: why would the floor sponsor's own chamber let it die after the House passed it 55-11?
H.B. 37 (2025) — absent on the deciding vote D
Power — The bill lowered the number of people needed to form a new town. That feeds the fast-track for "preliminary municipalities" we cover elsewhere. He did not vote. The final vote was close (39-30), close enough that his vote could have mattered.
Transparency — Missing a close, disputed vote gives the public nothing to check. There is no yes to defend and no no to explain.
Financial accountability — Not directly applicable.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — People in unincorporated areas affected by the lower number had no way to know where their own representative stood.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterShowing up and voting no would have cost him no more than being absent. And it would have given voters a real position to judge him by.
H.B. 17 (2026) — a narrow exception swapped for a broad one D+
RESOLVED, from the bill's own floor-amendment markup — The first version let a public infrastructure district (PID) board meet outside its own area only for a yearly "retreat." That meant once a year, for planning or training, as a public meeting. The board could not take final action or vote on anything except ending the meeting. A floor amendment struck that whole rule. In its place is one line: a board may meet anywhere "within the boundaries of the creating entity." There is no yearly limit, no limit on purpose and no ban on real votes. Every recorded vote on this bill was close to unanimous (committee 11-0-1, House floor 70-0-5, Senate 26-0-3). So Teuscher voted yes on the change.
Power — A PID board can now meet, and take binding votes, anywhere in a whole city or county. Before, it was only a once-a-year retreat with no voting.
Transparency — On its face this looks like "closing a loophole," because the retreat rule was struck. But what it really removed were the SAFEGUARDS on meeting outside the district, not the ability to do it.
Financial accountability — Not directly applicable to this specific provision.
Environmental impact — Not applicable.
Community impact — Many PIDs on empty land have no residents of their own, so people already have little say. This makes it easier for board meetings to happen even farther from anyone affected.
How This Could Have Served the Collective BetterKeep the retreat rule's real safeguards (once a year, no final votes) and only widen where it can be held. Do not trade a narrow, safe exception for a broad one with no limits.
